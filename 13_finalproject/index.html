<div class="textcontainer"> <p class="margin"></p> <h3>Final Project: A Pressure-sensing and corrective insole</h3> <p class="margin"></p> <p class="margin"></p> <div class="flexrow"> <iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/xuObYkerm98?si=5zo5ZUoyZ0IZLmeN" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" allowfullscreen> </iframe> </div> <p class="margin"></p> <p class="margin"></p> <div class="flexrow"> <a id="btn" href="finalproject.zip" download>Download the files used for my final project! </a> </div> <p class="margin"></p> <h4>Phase 1: Ideation</h4> <p class="margin"></p> The motivation for this project arose from my knowledge of complications in sports medicine and phyical therapy, where cases of individuals requiring corrective surgery or suffering from preventable back and/or knee problems due to years of misbalanced gait are entirely too common. I wanted to build a sole that could adaptively respond to the unique pressure pattern that users impressed into it, and change shape to redistribute pressure in a more balanced way. This was about as far as I had gotten during the brainstorming phase, still being unaware of what to use as an end effector and how to adjust the shape of the sole, as well as what materials to make it out of, and how to machine it. <p class="margin"></p> Early ideas of the end effector included some kind of aeration or intubation into foam, but I didn't find a way to do that in a way that would be effective under the weight of an entire human body. Another idea was to use servo motors to pull on a string to bend the fabric, but the servos weren't nearly powerful or resilient enough to make that much of an impact without getting fried. <p class="margin"></p> <p class="margin"></p> <h4>Phase 2: Prototyping</h4> <p class="margin"></p> I came up with a design for a four-sensor circuit and 3d-printed a thin mold for the sole after some testing, that used adhesive copper tape as the wiring instead of actual wire which maintained a surprising amount of flexibility and mobility within the sole, as well as good spatial resolution on the critical points of the shoe without setting up for too tedious of a wiring job. I anticipated at this point that the final design would be made out of foam, leather, or some kind of thin composite. Sole design is attached below: During the prototyping phase, I hadn't actually managed to solve the actuator problem. I was able to machine the body of the sole, as well as make functional voltage dividers out of force-sensitive resistors, and even calibrate them to certain outputs, first using LEDs and then various motor types. But all methods of sole deformation failed under weight, despite successful testing, as shown below: <iframe width="315" height="560" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/vERe63yoY6s?is=WCuqkfI4anB7ARoq" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen> </iframe> <p class="margin"></p> What's being shown above isn't explicitly obvious. I ended up going with four pressure sensors. One on the toe, one on the heel, one on the ball of the foot (inside) and one on the arch (outside). My code functions in a way such that, if there is a time-delayed difference between a peak in pressure on the toe and pressure on the heel that exceeds a certain threshold, the motor corresponding to the lesser-pressed of the two will vibrate, prompting the user to change their gait and redistribute pressure. This worked similarly across the arch-ball axis, except I used a smaller time delay. I found that this positive reinforcement loop was more satisfying than the reverse (i.e. vibrating the motor on the joint with too much pressure) since that felt like an electric shock and more of an irritant than anything else. This implementation felt "collaborative" and clinical, like I was learning from the sole instead of fighting it. <p class="margin"></p> <p class="margin"></p> <h4>Phase 3: Development and finalizing</h4> <p class="margin"></p> After this point, I was able to vacuum-form the sole such that the fabric stuck togther. This step was very frequently delayed because of the wiring constantly falling apart under flexion. I didn't find a better solution than using copper tape, but soldering wires to it made it incredibly fragile. This was soothed in some proportion by using foam and leather as opposed to, for example, silicone. Also, the foam layer underneath the sole allowed me to use DC motors since the foam was robust enough to withstand the weight of a person. It also gave me increased spatial resolution on the sensors, which helped in calibration, since now I was getting non-zero readings from pressure that wasn't precisely on the sensors. <p class="margin"></p> <div class="flexrow"> <img src="fp-mctest.png" alt="a breadboard with an Arduino and a single button connected"> </div> <p class="caption">I set up a test circuit with just the ItsyBitsy and a single button to make sure my wiring logic was correct.</p> <p class="margin"></p> Soldering this circuit proved very challenging, since I had to use multiple different chips. The XIAO didn't have enough ADC1 output pins for me to use four motors, so I had to switch boards not to a standard ESP32, which was too wide for any of the breadboards or protoboards, but to an Espressif (ESP32 S3 WROOM-1). This caused an issue becauseit didn't fit into the protoboard I was using until I tilted my sockets outwards. Also, recalibrating the MPU-6050 after soldering the board and placing it inside the housing did not yield meaningful results, since it had to be recalibrated every time the sole was placed into a shoe. <p class="margin"></p> After I finished building the sole, I was able to use a python file to chart the individual pressure values for each of the four sensors and export the results into a .csv file to build a 'user profile' for the wearer, and obtain a results profile where one could compare sensor values not just across spatial axes but across time. <p class="margin"></p> <div class="flexrow"> <img src="buttondetail.png" alt="close-up photo of a button with the outer shell removed"> </div> <div class="flexrow"> <img src="buttondetail3.png" alt="close-up photo of a button mechanism with the circuit open"> <img src="buttondetail4.png" alt="close-up photo of a button mechanism with the circuit closed"> </div> <p class="caption">Looking at the button mechanism revealed that this was a hardware problem, not a software one.</p> <p class="margin"></p> <p class="margin"></p>
